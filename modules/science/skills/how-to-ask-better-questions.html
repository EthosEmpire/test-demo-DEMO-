How to Ask Better Questions

Skill · 5 min · Science Module

Science advances by asking better questions. A vague or poorly framed question produces no useful answers. A precise, testable question opens the door to discovery. This skill is not just for scientists — it is the foundation of critical thinking in every domain.

What Makes a Question Good?

1. It is specific

"Does exercise improve health?" is too vague. "Does 30 minutes of aerobic exercise three times per week reduce resting heart rate in adults aged 30–50 over 8 weeks?" is specific enough to test.

2. It is testable

A good question can be investigated with evidence. "What is the meaning of life?" is not a scientific question. "Do people who report having clear life goals score higher on wellbeing assessments?" is testable.

3. It is falsifiable

You must be able to imagine what evidence would prove it wrong. If no evidence could ever disprove it, it is not a scientific question — it is a belief. Ask: "What would I have to see to conclude this is false?"

The Question Hierarchy

When studying a topic, questions have different levels of depth:

  • What? — describes a fact. "What is kinetic energy?" Surface level.
  • How? — describes a mechanism. "How does kinetic energy change when speed doubles?" Deeper.
  • Why? — seeks explanation. "Why does kinetic energy increase as a square of speed rather than linearly?" Deepest.
  • What if? — applies understanding. "What would happen to a car's stopping distance if the speed doubled?" Application — the highest level.

When reading, push your questions down this hierarchy. Most people stop at "What?" The real understanding lives in "How?", "Why?", and "What if?"

The 5-Why Technique

Ask "why?" five times in succession to get to the root of something.

Example: Why do plants grow toward light?

  1. Why? They need light for photosynthesis.
  2. Why? Photosynthesis converts light into glucose.
  3. Why? Glucose provides the energy and carbon for growth.
  4. Why? Plants cannot move to find food like animals — they must convert available energy sources in place.
  5. Why? Plants evolved to be stationary and must maximize sunlight capture to survive.

By the fifth why, you understand not just the fact but the evolutionary and chemical reason behind it. Apply this to anything you study.

Questions to Ask When Reading Science

  • "What evidence supports this claim?"
  • "What would disprove this?"
  • "What assumptions is this based on?"
  • "How was this tested? Was there a control group?"
  • "What are the limitations of this study?"
  • "Does this apply broadly or only in specific conditions?"
  • "Where does this contradict what I already know? Which is more supported by evidence?"
Practice Task: Take the last scientific claim you encountered (from a news article, social media, conversation, or class). Apply the questions above to it. How well does the claim hold up?
Reflection: In which area of your life do you ask the fewest questions and just accept what you are told? What would happen if you applied scientific-level questioning there?
