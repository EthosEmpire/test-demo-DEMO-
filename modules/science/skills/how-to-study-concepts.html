How to Study Concepts (Not Memorize)

Skill · 5 min · Science Module

Most students study by reading and re-reading, highlighting, and trying to remember. This is one of the least effective study methods ever tested. Understanding is not the same as memorization, and understanding produces results that last.

The Difference Between Understanding and Memorizing

Memorizing is storing a fact: "Newton's Second Law is F = ma."
Understanding is knowing what it means and why: "If I double the force on an object, it accelerates twice as fast. If I double the object's mass, I need double the force to achieve the same acceleration."

Understanding means you can apply the concept to a new problem you have never seen before. Memorized facts alone cannot do this. Every science exam that matters tests understanding, not recall.

How to Actually Study a Concept

1
Read for the idea, not the words

When reading a chapter, your goal is to answer: "What is the main idea here and why is it true?" Not: "What sentences might be on the exam?" Read actively — stop after each paragraph and ask yourself what you just learned.

2
Close the book and write it out

After reading a section, close it. Write down what you remember in your own words. Do not copy sentences. Translate the concept into language a 12-year-old would understand. The gaps in what you can write are exactly what you need to reread and study.

3
Find the mechanism, not just the outcome

Do not just know that "plants need sunlight to grow." Know why: photosynthesis converts light energy into glucose that the plant uses for energy and building material. Ask "why?" and "how?" for every fact you encounter. The answer reveals the mechanism, and mechanisms are what you actually understand.

4
Make connections to things you already know

New knowledge sticks when you connect it to existing knowledge. Kinetic energy increases with speed — same as why a faster car causes more damage in a crash than a slower one. Energy is conserved — same principle as why a ball thrown upward slows down before coming back. Look for analogies everywhere.

5
Solve problems without looking at examples first

The temptation is to look at a solved example, think "I get it," and move on. This creates the illusion of understanding. Instead: read the concept, close the book, attempt the problem. Struggle is where learning happens. Only look at the solution after you have genuinely tried.

Spaced Repetition

Your brain forgets. Without review, you will forget 50% of new material within a day and 70% within a week. Spaced repetition fights this by reviewing material at increasing intervals: day 1, day 3, day 7, day 21. Tools like Anki automate this. The key insight: reviewing 15 minutes daily beats one 3-hour session weekly for long-term retention.
